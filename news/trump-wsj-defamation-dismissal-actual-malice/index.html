Trump’s Wall Street Journal Suit Dismissed: Why a Denial Was Not Enough

Does publishing a disputed story after its subject denies it establish actual malice?

Analysis

Published by The Legal Circle

November 1, 2026

United States; Canadian defamation-law comparison

Does publishing a disputed story after its subject denies it establish actual malice?

A federal judge has rejected that argument in President Donald Trump’s defamation lawsuit against The Wall Street Journal. On October 31, U.S. District Judge Darrin Gayles dismissed Trump’s amended complaint with prejudice. The dispute concerns reporting about a letter attributed to Trump in Jeffrey Epstein’s birthday album. Trump denies writing it. The dismissal did not establish who wrote the letter or whether the reporting was true.

For lawyers, the distinction is the story: a claim can fail at the pleading stage without the court resolving the underlying factual dispute.

What the actual-malice standard required

The court required factual allegations plausibly supporting knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth. It found the amended complaint insufficient. Publishing despite Trump’s denial did not, by itself, satisfy that standard; the article also included his denial.

That separates two questions a prospective plaintiff must confront: “Was the account false?” and “What supports an inference about the publisher’s state of mind?”

Treating those questions as interchangeable risks building a complaint around the intensity of the dispute rather than the legal element that must be pleaded.

What the court left unresolved

Gayles expressly declined to determine whether the article was true or whether Trump authored the letter. The court also left attorneys’ fees and costs for further consideration, rather than awarding them in the dismissal order.

The practical reporting lesson is equally clear. “The lawsuit was dismissed” accurately describes the outcome. “The court authenticated the letter” does not.

What happens next?

Dismissal with prejudice closes the opportunity to amend these claims again in district court, subject to appellate review. Trump’s legal team has said it intends to appeal. That statement should not be reported as confirmation that an appeal has already been filed.

For Canadian practitioners following the litigation, this is a U.S. pleading decision, not a statement of Canadian defamation law. Its comparative value lies in the discipline it demands: identify the procedural stage, isolate the element the court decided and resist turning an unsuccessful claim into a factual verdict.

The complaint failed. The authorship question remained unanswered.

Sources: October 31 dismissal order, ECF 80; Federal court opinion record; Reuters, October 31; Reuters syndicated report; Associated Press, October 31; Washington Post.
