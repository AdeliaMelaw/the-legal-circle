November 12, 2026

European Union; Canadian business implications

Legislative proposal

An app can influence what a consumer spends long before checkout. It can make a free trial easy to start and hard to cancel, obscure the real price of an in-game purchase, or keep someone engaged with prompts they cannot easily turn off. The European Commission’s Digital Fairness Act proposal would give regulators more specific rules for examining those choices.

The Commission adopted and published its proposal on November 11, 2026. The proposed regulation, COM(2026) 1100 final, would replace the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. It is not law in force and can change as the European Parliament and Council consider it.

Product decision Existing EU framework, in brief What the proposal would add
Designing a checkout or interface General unfair commercial practices rules apply; separate platform rules can also be relevant More explicit restrictions on specified manipulative designs and misleading prompts
Targeting ads or setting personalised prices Consumer, privacy and platform rules can overlap Targeted restrictions involving minors and the exploitation of consumer vulnerabilities
Selling virtual items or paid random rewards General consumer protections still apply Real-money price disclosures, information about reward odds, and restrictions involving minors, subject to exceptions
Using features that encourage excessive use Applicable duties depend on the service and the rules governing it Controls to disable specified features, with scope limits and exceptions
Converting a free trial into a paid contract Consumer contract and disclosure rules apply A reminder and active acceptance before conversion

The proposal also addresses influencer disclosures and pricing transparency. Its dynamic-pricing provisions include specific measures for event tickets and virtual queues; they should not be read as a blanket prohibition on changing prices.

The proposed 6% fine ceiling comes from a separate text. Article 43 of the proposed Consumer Protection Cooperation regulation would allow the Commission to impose fines of up to 6% of a trader’s worldwide annual turnover for specified intentional or negligent breaches in proceedings under that framework. It is not an automatic penalty for every Digital Fairness Act violation.

For Canadian businesses selling to EU consumers, the useful first step is to map how customers actually encounter the product: which settings are on by default, how prices appear, how trials convert and how customers leave. The published proposal gives legal and product teams something concrete to compare with those flows while the legislation is still being negotiated.

Sources: European Commission proposal page · Proposed Digital Fairness Act, COM(2026) 1100 final · Companion Consumer Protection Cooperation proposal, Article 43
