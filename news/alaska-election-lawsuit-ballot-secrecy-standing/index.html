Alaska Election Lawsuit Dismissed: Why Ballot Secrecy Defeated DOJ’s Proposed Remedy

A ballot records a choice. Can a court investigate whether the voter meant to choose someone else?

That question sits at the centre of a federal lawsuit over two similarly named candidates in Alaska’s U.S. Senate election: incumbent Senator Dan S. Sullivan and challenger Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. On November 1, Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the Justice Department’s amended complaint for lack of standing. The government appealed that day.

The decision offers a practical litigation lesson: alleging an electoral problem is not enough. A plaintiff must also identify relief the court can lawfully and effectively provide.

What the government wanted

The Justice Department sought to prevent certification of a result potentially affected by voters confusing the candidates, pending an investigation into their intentions. Its theory depended on the incumbent losing when reallocating votes cast for the other Sullivan could have changed the outcome. Those assertions were allegations, not findings that voters had made mistakes.

The distinction matters. A vote for a similarly named candidate does not, by itself, reveal an error. Treating the selection as suspicious would require a method for distinguishing deliberate choices from mistaken ones.

Why the proposed remedy failed

Gleason found that the government had supplied no workable way to investigate voter intent while preserving ballot secrecy. Already-cast ballots had been mixed together, and the government had not explained how its proposed intervention could overcome the secrecy problem. The failure to establish redressability meant the court lacked jurisdiction.

For litigators, that places remedy design at the beginning of the case. A request to pause an outcome pending an investigation still needs an explanation of how that investigation could produce a lawful remedy.

The judgment therefore concerns the court’s power to address the alleged injury. It does not establish that the candidates’ similar names caused confusion - or determine responsibility for any alleged attempt to engineer it.

Without prejudice, but without another amendment

The court dismissed the complaint without prejudice, reflecting its lack of jurisdiction to decide the merits. It nevertheless refused permission to amend again and directed that the case be closed. Gleason also cited the caution against changing election procedures immediately before voting concludes.

Those procedural descriptions belong together. “Without prejudice” should not be presented as an invitation to submit another version of the complaint in the same proceeding.

The appeal remains separate

The government filed a notice of appeal on November 1. Subsequent tracking identifies the Ninth Circuit proceeding as No. 26-7304. No appellate ruling reversing the dismissal was identified in this review.

For Canadian lawyers, the comparative value is the relationship between jurisdiction, evidence and remedies - not the transplantation of U.S. standing doctrine into Canadian law.

A court cannot investigate what a voter meant without first confronting what a secret ballot protects.
